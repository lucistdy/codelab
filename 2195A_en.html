Sieve of Erato67henes: Hunting for a Prime in Disguise

You're handed a tiny bag of up to 5 numbers, each between 1 and 67. Can you pick out some of them — maybe just one, maybe several — so that when you multiply your picks together, you get exactly 67? It sounds like it could involve tricky combinations… until you notice one thing about the number 67 itself that makes the whole problem collapse into something almost embarrassingly simple.

LEVEL 1

The mission

You're given n positive whole numbers (1 ≤ n ≤ 5), each one between 1 and 67. You get to pick any subset of them — at least one, but you don't have to use them all — and multiply your picks together. Your job: decide whether some choice of picks makes the product come out to exactly 67.

You'll be asked this for up to 10,000 separate test cases in one run, but each one only has at most 5 numbers to work with — tiny!

Straight from the judge

[1, 7, 6, 7, 67] → YES. Pick the 1st and 5th numbers: 1 × 67 = 67.
[1, 3, 5, 7, 8] → NO. No matter which numbers you pick from this bag, you can't make the product land on exactly 67.

A tempting (wrong) first instinct

With only 5 numbers, you might think: "easy, I'll just try every possible combination of picks and check each one." That would actually work here — but before reaching for brute force, let's look closer at the target number itself. Sometimes a number has a special personality that makes the whole problem trivial once you notice it.

LEVEL 2

67 has a secret identity: it's prime

Check it yourself: does anything from 2 through 66 divide evenly into 67? Try a few — 67 ÷ 2, 67 ÷ 3, 67 ÷ 7… none of them come out even. 67 is a prime number. That means its only two positive whole-number factors are 1 and 67 itself.

Why does that matter here? Because our whole puzzle is really a question about factoring: "can I build 67 by multiplying some numbers together?" is exactly the same question as "what are the ways to write 67 as a product of positive integers?"

Cold Case File

Need a refresher on what makes a number prime, or how to test for it? The Prime Number Detective Agency page builds up primality testing from scratch. This problem leans on the flip side of that idea: primes are numbers that refuse to be built out of smaller pieces (other than 1).

LEVEL 3

Every way to build 67 out of whole numbers

Let's list every way to write 67 as a product of two or more positive integers, each at least 1. Since 67's only factors are 1 and 67, here's what happens when you try:

  • 67 = 67 — just the number itself, no multiplying needed.
  • 67 = 1 × 67 — works, since 1 doesn't change anything.
  • 67 = 1 × 1 × 67, 67 = 1 × 1 × 1 × 67, … — still works, you can toss in as many extra 1's as you like.
  • 67 = a × b where both a and b are greater than 1? Impossible — that would mean 67 has a factor other than 1 and itself, contradicting that it's prime!

So every single way to multiply positive integers together and land on 67 requires exactly one factor equal to 67, and every other factor involved must be exactly 1 (any number of them, including zero extra 1's).

LEVEL 4

The whole problem in one sentence

Translate Level 3 back into the language of "picking numbers from your bag": since every winning selection needs exactly one 67 and any number of 1's (which you're even free to leave out — picking just {67} alone always works on its own), the entire problem boils down to a single yes/no question about the bag you were handed.

The one sentence that matters most

The answer is YES if and only if the number 67 appears anywhere in the list — and every other number in the bag is completely irrelevant to the answer!

That's the whole "aha" of this problem. It looks like it might involve clever combinations of several numbers multiplying together in interesting ways — but because the target is prime, it collapses down to a single membership check. No multiplication required at solve-time at all.

LEVEL 8

Practice: crack these before you check with the tool

Work each one out on paper first — then verify with the widget above.

Exercise 1: [2, 67, 5]. What's the answer, and which single number decides it?

YES — the list contains 67 (the second entry), so picking just {67} alone gives a product of exactly 67. The 2 and the 5 are completely irrelevant.

Exercise 2: [1, 1, 1, 1]. Explain in one sentence why no selection can ever reach 67.

NO — every number in the bag is 1, and multiplying any collection of 1's together (in any amount) can only ever produce 1, never 67.

Exercise 3: [2, 3, 5, 7, 11]. These are all prime numbers — does that help you build 67? Why or why not?

NO. Even though every number here is prime, that doesn't help build a different prime. Multiplying any subset of {2,3,5,7,11} gives a number whose only prime factors come from that same set — and 67 is a completely different prime, so it can never appear as a product of these. (This is the flip side of Level 2: multiplying primes together builds new numbers with those primes as factors, and 67 just isn't reachable this way.)

Exercise 4 (edge case): [67]. With only one number in the whole bag, is the answer YES or NO?

YES. You're always allowed to select just one element (remember, you can't select zero elements, but selecting exactly one is completely fine) — and picking that single 67 gives a product of exactly 67.

Exercise 5 (challenge): Modify the code to also print which selected numbers achieve the product, instead of just YES/NO.
erato67henes_verbose.py
t = int(input())
for _ in range(t):
    n = int(input())
    a = list(map(int, input().split()))
    if 67 in a:
        idx = a.index(67)
        print("YES, pick just a" + str(idx + 1) + " (the value 67)")
    else:
        print("NO")
