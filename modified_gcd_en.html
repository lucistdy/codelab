Modified GCD: Search Inside a Range

You already know how to find the single biggest common divisor of two numbers. Codeforces 75C asks something trickier: out of all the numbers a and b have in common, what's the biggest one that also fits inside a given range — and can you answer that question 10,000 times without breaking a sweat?

No math background needed Precompute once, answer fast Interactive
LEVEL 1

The mission

You're given two numbers, a and b. Then you're handed n separate questions, each one asking: what's the biggest number that divides evenly into both a and b, while also being between low and high (inclusive)? If no such number exists, the answer is -1.

Straight from the judge

a = 9, b = 27, with 3 queries:
low=1, high=5 → 3 (3 divides both 9 and 27, and it's the biggest such number in [1, 5]).
low=10, high=11 → -1 (no common divisor of 9 and 27 lands in [10, 11]).
low=9, high=11 → 9 (9 divides both numbers, and it fits in [9, 11]).

Why should you care?

a and b can be as large as 1,000,000,000, and you can be asked up to 10,000 queries. If you re-solve the whole problem from scratch for every single query, you'll still be computing when the judge's patience runs out. The trick is to do the hard work exactly once, then answer every query almost instantly.

LEVEL 2

The one fact that unlocks everything

Here's the fact this whole problem is built on: every common divisor of a and b is a divisor of gcd(a, b) — and every divisor of gcd(a, b) is a common divisor of a and b. They're exactly the same set of numbers.

Check it with the sample: gcd(9, 27) = 9. The divisors of 9 are 1, 3, 9. Now list the divisors of 9 and 27 separately: divisors of 9 are 1, 3, 9; divisors of 27 are 1, 3, 9, 27. The numbers they have in common? 1, 3, 9 — exactly the divisors of the gcd. No coincidence.

Cold Case File

Haven't met GCD yet, or want a refresher on what makes it special? The Maximum GCD page covers the basics from scratch — this page picks up right where that one leaves off.

So instead of hunting for common divisors of two possibly-huge numbers directly, we only ever need to study one number: g = gcd(a, b). Every one of its divisors is automatically a common divisor of a and b, and there are no others hiding anywhere.

LEVEL 3

Why not just scan each range directly?

The obvious plan for a single query: walk through every number from low to high, check whether it divides both a and b, and remember the biggest one that does.

The problem: high − low can be nearly 1,000,000,000 on its own, and you might face 10,000 queries like that. Redoing a billion-sized scan ten thousand times is a plan that only works if you enjoy watching progress bars.

LEVEL 4

The key insight: find every divisor of g, once

Since every common divisor of a and b is a divisor of g = gcd(a, b), all we need is a complete list of g's divisors. And there's a neat shortcut for building that list fast: divisors always come in pairs.

small divisor
i
↔pairs with
large divisor
g / i

If i divides g evenly, so does g/i — and one of that pair is always ≤ √g. So checking every i up to √g finds every divisor, both halves of every pair.

For g up to 1,000,000,000, √g is only about 31,623 — a loop that finishes almost instantly, even though it's checking a number as big as a billion. Once you have every divisor, sort them from smallest to biggest.

The one sentence that matters most

Do the expensive work — finding gcd(a, b) and every one of its divisors — exactly once. After that, each query is just a quick look through a short, sorted list.

Answering a query is then simple: walk the sorted divisor list from the biggest divisor down to the smallest, and stop at the first one that's ≤ high. If that divisor is also ≥ low, it's your answer. If it's smaller than low, then nothing in the whole list can possibly be in range — every other divisor is even smaller — so the answer is -1.

LEVEL 5

Reading the code, line by line

Here's the entire solution. Don't worry about understanding it all at once — read the code on the left, then the plain-English translation on the right.

modified_gcd.py
def gcd(x, y):
    while y:
        x, y = y, x % y
    return x

def divisors_of(g):
    divs = []
    i = 1
    while i * i <= g:
        if g % i == 0:
            divs.append(i)
            if i != g // i:
                divs.append(g // i)
        i += 1
    divs.sort()
    return divs

a, b = map(int, input().split())
g = gcd(a, b)
divs = divisors_of(g)   # every common divisor of a and b, smallest to biggest

n = int(input())
for _ in range(n):
    low, high = map(int, input().split())
    answer = -1
    for d in reversed(divs):   # biggest divisor first
        if d <= high:
            if d >= low:
                answer = d
            break
    print(answer)

Let's trace it together: a=9, b=27

gcd(9, 27) = 9, and its divisors, sorted, are [1, 3, 9]. Let's answer all three sample queries by scanning from the biggest divisor down.

Matches the judge's expected output exactly: 3, -1, 9

Notice query 2: the very first divisor that fits under high=11 is 9, but 9 < 10 = low. Since every other divisor (3, 1) is even smaller, none of them could possibly be ≥ 10 either — so we can stop immediately and report -1 without checking further.

Challenge 2: a=12, b=18, query low=4, high=5. What's the answer?

gcd(12, 18) = 6. Divisors of 6: [1, 2, 3, 6]. Scanning from the top: 6 ≤ 5? No, skip. 3 ≤ 5? Yes — but 3 ≥ 4? No. Stop here (every remaining divisor is even smaller). Answer: -1 — there's simply no common divisor of 12 and 18 sitting between 4 and 5.

Challenge 3: Why is it true that every common divisor of a and b must divide gcd(a, b)?

Think of it this way: gcd(a, b) is the biggest number that divides evenly into both a and b — it's built entirely out of the "shared ingredients" of a and b. Any other common divisor of a and b is made from a smaller piece of those same shared ingredients, and a smaller piece of something always divides evenly into the whole thing. That's exactly why every common divisor shows up on gcd(a, b)'s own list of divisors, and nothing else ever sneaks in.
