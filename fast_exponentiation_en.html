LEVEL 1

The problem: multiplying is slow, one at a time

Here's the mission: compute 2 raised to the power of 1,000,000. That means multiplying 2 × 2 × 2 × 2… a million times in a row.

If a computer does this the obvious way — multiply by 2, then multiply by 2 again, then again — it needs one multiplication for every single step. Want 210? That's 9 multiplications. Want 21,000,000? That's 999,999 multiplications, one after another, no shortcuts.

Why should you care?

This isn't just a math-class curiosity. The secret codes that protect your passwords, your messages, and your online shopping (you'll meet this later in the case file as RSA-style cryptography) all depend on raising huge numbers to huge powers. If computers actually needed a million steps every time, the internet would grind to a halt. They don't — because of the trick on this page.

So here's the challenge for this lesson: can we compute 21,000,000 using way fewer than a million multiplications? Spoiler — yes. About 20 will do it. Let's find out how.

LEVEL 2

The big idea: squaring skips ahead

You already know this trick from somewhere else — cell division. One cell splits into 2. Those 2 split into 4. Those 4 split into 8. Every round, the amount doubles, and the numbers explode fast: after just 20 rounds you've already blown past a million cells.

Exponents work the exact same way, if you look at them the right way. Watch what happens when you only ever square a number (multiply it by itself):

Look closely: to get from a¹ all the way to a⁸ (that's a to the 8th power), we only needed 3 multiplications — not 7! Every time you square, you don't add one more copy of a, you double the exponent. 1 → 2 → 4 → 8. That's the same doubling speed as the cell-division example, and it's why the numbers you can reach explode just as fast: 20 squarings gets you all the way to the exponent 220, which is over a million.

The one sentence that matters most

Multiplying by a adds 1 to the exponent. Squaring doubles the exponent. Doubling gets big way, way faster than adding — that's the entire secret.

LEVEL 3

But what about exponents like 13? Meet binary

Squaring alone only reaches exponents that are exact powers of 2: 1, 2, 4, 8, 16, 32… But we don't just want a8, we might want a13. 13 isn't a power of 2. So how do we get there using only squarings?

Here's the trick: any whole number can be built by adding up powers of 2. This is exactly what "binary" means. Take 13:

13 = 8 + 4 + 1. In "binary," we write that as 1101 — a 1 under every power of 2 we're using (8, 4, 1), and a 0 under the one we're skipping (2).

Now here's why that matters for exponents. A basic rule of powers is that multiplying two powers of the same number adds their exponents: a8 × a4 × a1 = a8+4+1 = a13. So instead of multiplying a by itself 12 times, we can:

  1. Build a¹, a², a⁴, a⁸ by squaring — only 3 multiplications
  2. Multiply together the ones that match the 1s in 1101: that's a⁸, a⁴, and a¹ — 2 more multiplications

Total: 5 multiplications to compute a13, instead of 12. And the bigger the exponent gets, the more dramatic this saving becomes — because binary numbers grow their digit-count much slower than the number itself. That's the whole magic trick.

LEVEL 4

A quick word about "mod" (the clock trick)

The real code doesn't just compute huge powers — it also does something called "mod m," meaning "give me the remainder after dividing by m." You've used this your whole life without a fancy name for it: it's clock math.

A clock only has 12 numbers. If it's 10 o'clock and 5 hours pass, you don't say "15 o'clock" — you wrap back around to 3 o'clock. That's exactly 15 mod 12 = 3. Once you hit the top, you loop back to the start.

In our code, every single multiplication is immediately followed by "% m" (take the remainder). That keeps every number in the calculation small and manageable — always between 0 and m − 1 — even if the "real" answer, without wrapping around, would have hundreds of digits. This is exactly why the trick can handle exponents like a million: the numbers never get the chance to blow up.

LEVEL 5

Reading the actual code, line by line

Here's the real function from the case file. Don't worry about understanding it all at once — read the code on the left, then the plain-English translation on the right.

fast_power.py
def fast_power(a, b, m):
    # computes (a to the power of b) % m, without ever building that giant number
    result = 1
    base = a % m
    while b:                      # stop once b hits 0
        if b % 2 == 1:
            result = result * base % m
        base = base * base % m    # this line is the "doubling"
        b = b // 2
    return result

print(fast_power(2, 1000000, 1000000007))
result = 1 base = a % m
result starts empty (multiplying by 1 changes nothing). base starts as our very first power, a¹ — already clock-wrapped by m.
while b:
Keep going as long as there's more of the exponent left to process. Each lap through the loop handles one binary digit of b.
if b % 2 == 1: result = result * base % m
Is the digit we're looking at a 1? If so, this power belongs in the final answer — multiply it in. If it's a 0, skip it, we don't need it.
base = base * base % m
This is the doubling move from Level 2: square base so it becomes the next power — a¹ becomes a², a² becomes a⁴, a⁴ becomes a⁸…
b = b // 2
Chop the exponent in half (drop the digit we just used). This shrinks b to 0 in about log₂(b) steps — for a million, that's only ~20.
Cold Case File

Python already has this superpower built in — you don't need to write it yourself: pow(a, b, m) is a built-in function, and that third argument tells it "take the remainder along the way," using exactly the fast exponentiation trick you just learned, under the hood!

Challenge 3: Why does the loop only take about log₂(b) rounds instead of b rounds?

Every single round divides b by 2 (b = b // 2). Starting from a big number and cutting it in half over and over is exactly what "log₂" measures — it's asking "how many times can I cut this in half before I hit zero?" For b = 1,000,000 the answer is about 20, because 220 is already over a million. That's why 20 rounds is enough — each round is doing the work of doubling the previous one's reach.
