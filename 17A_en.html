The Noldbach Problem: When Prime Neighbors Team Up

Nick loves prime numbers so much that he invented his own math problem, named after a famous one. Codeforces 17A asks: if you take every pair of "neighboring" primes, add them together, and add 1 — how many prime numbers up to n can you build that way? And does that count reach at least k? This one isn't about outsmarting a huge number — it's about reading the rules *very* carefully.

LEVEL 1

The mission

You may have heard of the famous, still-unsolved Goldbach conjecture: every even number greater than 2 can be written as the sum of two primes (like 10 = 3 + 7). Nick liked that idea so much he invented his own version, which he named Noldbach's problem (get it? Nick's Goldbach):

Nick's claim

At least k prime numbers from 2 to n can be written as two neighboring primes, plus 1. For example: 19 = 7 + 11 + 1, or 13 = 5 + 7 + 1.

You're given two whole numbers, n and k. Your job: figure out whether Nick is right — print YES if at least k primes between 2 and n (inclusive) can be built this way, or NO if they can't.

Straight from the judge

n=27, k=2 → YES. Both 13 (= 5+7+1) and 19 (= 7+11+1) qualify, and 2 ≥ 2.
n=45, k=7 → NO. It turns out only 5 primes up to 45 can be built this way — not enough to reach 7.

LEVEL 2

What exactly does "neighboring" mean?

This is the single most important sentence in the whole problem, so let's slow down: "Two prime numbers are called neighboring if there are no other prime numbers between them." That means: line up every prime number in order — 2, 3, 5, 7, 11, 13, 17, 19, 23, … — and "neighboring" just means "next to each other in that list." It does not mean the two primes have to differ by exactly 2!

A trap worth avoiding

You might think "neighboring primes" means twin primes (pairs like 11 and 13 that differ by exactly 2). That's a common mix-up, but it's wrong here! 23 and 29 are neighboring primes too (nothing prime sits between them — 24, 25, 26, 27, 28 are all composite), even though they're 6 apart. "Neighboring" just means "next to each other on the list," no matter the size of the gap.

LEVEL 3

Checking the examples by hand

Let's confirm the two examples the note gives us actually work, using the definition from Level 2.

  • 5 and 7 are neighboring primes (nothing prime sits at 6). 5 + 7 + 1 = 13, and 13 is itself prime. So 13 is "Noldbach-expressible."
  • 7 and 11 are neighboring primes (nothing prime sits at 8, 9, or 10). 7 + 11 + 1 = 19, and 19 is itself prime. So 19 is "Noldbach-expressible" too.

Notice the pattern: we're not just adding two neighboring primes and checking if that sum is interesting — we add 1 to the sum, and then check whether that final result happens to also be a prime number sitting somewhere in our 2-to-n range. Two separate primality checks are secretly hiding in this problem: the two ingredients must be neighboring primes, and the result must be prime too.

LEVEL 4

Our trusty tool: the Sieve of Eratosthenes

Since n only goes up to 1,000 here, this problem isn't about outrunning a huge number like some of the puzzles you may have solved before — it's refreshingly small! But we still need a fast, reliable way to find every prime from 2 to n, and to check "is this number prime?" instantly, over and over.

That's exactly the job of the Sieve of Eratosthenes: sweep through the numbers 2 to n once, and cross off every multiple of every prime you find, leaving only true primes standing. Building it takes barely any time at all for n up to 1,000.

LEVEL 5

The recipe

Now that we have every prime up to n on hand, here's the plan, step by step:

  1. Sieve to find every prime from 2 to n, and keep them in a sorted list.
  2. Walk down that list, looking at each pair of neighbors (the 1st and 2nd, the 2nd and 3rd, the 3rd and 4th, and so on).
  3. For each neighboring pair, compute their sum plus 1.
  4. Check: is that result itself a prime number? If yes, we've found one more "Noldbach-expressible" prime — remember it.
  5. After checking every neighboring pair, count how many distinct primes we found this way, and compare that count to k.
The one sentence that matters most

Walk every neighboring pair of primes once, test whether "sum + 1" is prime, and count how many different primes that test successfully hits. If the count is at least k, the answer is YES.

LEVEL 6

Reading the code, line by line

Here's the entire solution. Read the code on the left, then the plain-English translation on the right.

noldbach.py
n, k = map(int, input().split())

is_prime = [True] * (n + 1)
is_prime[0] = is_prime[1] = False
for i in range(2, int(n ** 0.5) + 1):
    if is_prime[i]:
        for j in range(i * i, n + 1, i):
            is_prime[j] = False

primes = [i for i in range(2, n + 1) if is_prime[i]]

found = set()
for i in range(len(primes) - 1):
    total = primes[i] + primes[i + 1] + 1
    if total <= n and is_prime[total]:
        found.add(total)

print("YES" if len(found) >= k else "NO")

Practice: crack these before you check with the tool

Exercise 1: n=13, k=1. Which single prime pair makes this work, and why is the answer YES?

Primes up to 13: 2,3,5,7,11,13. Checking neighbor sums: 2+3+1=6 (no), 3+5+1=9 (no), 5+7+1=13 (yes! and 13 ≤ 13), 7+11+1=19 (too big, skip since 19>13), 11+13+1=25 (too big). Only 1 prime found (13), which meets k=1. Answer: YES.

Exercise 2: n=19, k=3. We already know 13 and 19 both qualify (Level 1). Is there a third one hiding below 19? Why is the answer NO?

Checking every neighboring pair with primes up to 19 (2,3,5,7,11,13,17,19): the only sums that land on a prime ≤19 are 5+7+1=13 and 7+11+1=19. The next pair, 11+13+1=25, isn't prime, and 13+17+1=31 is already past 19. So only 2 primes qualify, not 3. Answer: NO.

Exercise 3: n=20, k=2. Does raising n from 19 to 20 change anything about the count from Exercise 2?

No new primes appear between 19 and 20 (20 isn't prime), so the achievable set is still just {13, 19} — still 2. Since k=2 this time (not 3), 2 ≥ 2 holds. Answer: YES. This shows how sensitive these "at least k" problems are to the exact value of k!

Exercise 4 (challenge): n=1000, k=44. It turns out exactly 44 primes up to 1000 are Noldbach-expressible — not one more. What would the answer be for n=1000, k=45, and why?

For k=44, we have exactly enough (44 ≥ 44), so the answer is YES. But for k=45, there simply aren't enough qualifying primes below 1000 — the maximum achievable count for this n is 44, so no value of k above that can ever succeed. Answer: NO. This is a nice reminder that "at least k" problems can have a hard ceiling that no amount of searching can beat.
