Sequence of Balls: Rebuild the Necklace, Cheaply

You have a string of lettered balls, A, and you want to turn it into a different string of balls, B. You have four tools — insert, delete, replace, and swap two neighbors — and each one costs a different amount of time. What's the cheapest possible way to get from A to B?

No math background needed Dynamic programming Interactive
LEVEL 1

The mission, straight from the judge

Picture a string of lettered balls threaded on a necklace — that's sequence A. Your teacher wants you to turn it into a different necklace, B, using exactly four tools:

Insert
add any ball anywhere — costs ti
Delete
remove any ball — costs td
Replace
relabel any ball — costs tr
Exchange
swap two neighboring balls — costs te

You can use any tool as many times as you like, in any order. The question is simple to state and surprisingly tricky to answer: what's the minimum total time to turn A into B?

Straight from the judge

t_i=1, t_d=1, t_r=1, t_e=1, A=youshouldnot, B=thoushaltnot → 5
t_i=2, t_d=4, t_r=10, t_e=3, A=ab, B=ba → 3
t_i=1, t_d=10, t_r=20, t_e=30, A=a, B=za → 1

Heads up: this one's a real challenge

This is one of the hardest problems in this whole series — strings can be up to 4,000 balls long, so trying every possible sequence of moves is completely out of the question. We're going to build up the idea slowly, one small piece at a time, so don't worry if it feels like a lot at first. By the end, it'll click.

One rule the judge quietly guarantees

You're also told that 2×t_e ≥ t_i + t_d always holds. Keep that fact in your back pocket — it turns out to be the exact thing that makes this problem solvable at all. We'll see why in Level 5.

LEVEL 2

Warm-up: just insert and delete

Let's ignore replace and swap for a moment and pretend you only have two tools: insert and delete. Sample 3 is a perfect example: A = "a", B = "za", with t_i=1 and t_d=10.

z
a

The 'a' ball is already exactly where it needs to be — only a 'z' needs to be inserted in front of it.

The clumsy plan would be: delete the 'a' (cost 10), then insert 'z' and a fresh 'a' (cost 1+1=2), for a total of 12. But look closer — the 'a' in A is already sitting in the right spot relative to B! You don't need to touch it at all. Just insert a 'z' in front of it, for a total cost of just 1 — exactly what the judge expects.

The big idea

The smart way to solve these problems is to figure out which balls you can leave alone, and only pay for the balls that actually need to change. That means comparing A and B ball by ball, from the front, and asking: "does this next ball already match, or do I need a tool?"

To track "how much of A have I processed" and "how much of B have I built so far" at the same time, we'll build a grid: rows count how many balls of A we've consumed, columns count how many balls of B we've built. Each cell will hold the cheapest cost to get that far. This kind of grid-building technique has a name: dynamic programming — solving a big problem by combining answers to smaller versions of itself.

With only insert and delete, the rule for filling cell (i, j) — meaning "the first i balls of A turned into the first j balls of B" — is:

if A[i−1] == B[j−1]: dp[i][j] = dp[i−1][j−1] (free — they already match)
else: dp[i][j] = min(dp[i−1][j] + t_d, dp[i][j−1] + t_i)

LEVEL 3

Adding replace: the classic "edit distance"

Now bring back the third tool: replace. If the current balls don't match, instead of deleting one and inserting a different one (two operations), you could just relabel the ball in place (one operation) for cost t_r. Sometimes that's cheaper, sometimes it isn't — the grid figures out which, automatically.

The rule grows by exactly one option:

dp[i][j] = min(
  dp[i−1][j] + t_d,  delete A's ball
  dp[i][j−1] + t_i,  insert B's ball
  dp[i−1][j−1] + (0 if A[i−1]==B[j−1] else t_r)  keep or replace
)

You already know this algorithm's famous cousin

When t_i = t_d = t_r = 1, this exact grid computes something called the Levenshtein distance (also just called "edit distance") — the same idea spell-checkers and "Did you mean...?" suggestions use to measure how different two words are. Sample 1 (youshouldnot → thoushaltnot, all costs = 1) is a plain edit-distance question in disguise, and the answer is exactly 5.

LEVEL 4

The special move: swapping neighbors

Here's where this problem gets interesting. Look at sample 2: A = "ab", B = "ba", with t_i=2, t_d=4, t_r=10, t_e=3.

Using only the three tools from before, the cheapest plan is: delete 'a' from the front (cost 4), then insert a fresh 'a' at the end (cost 2) — total 6. Replacing both balls costs 2 × 10 = 20, even worse. But there's a move neither of those plans considers: just swap the two balls in place. That's a single exchange, for cost t_e = 3 — half the price of the delete-and-insert plan!

Straight from the judge's note

"In the second sample, you could delete the ball labeled 'a' from the first position and then insert another 'a' at the new second position with total time 6. However exchanging the balls gives total time 3."

So the grid needs a fourth option, but it only applies in a very specific situation: the last two balls of the piece of A you've used so far are the exact mirror image of the last two balls of the piece of B you've built so far. In symbols, for cell (i, j) with i ≥ 2 and j ≥ 2:

if A[i−1] == B[j−2] and A[i−2] == B[j−1]:
  you may also consider dp[i−2][j−2] + t_e

That's exactly what happens at the very last cell for "ab" → "ba": the last ball of A ('b') matches the second-to-last ball of B ('b'), and the second-to-last ball of A ('a') matches the last ball of B ('a'). A perfect adjacent swap.

LEVEL 5

The full recipe (and that one weird rule)

Putting all four tools together, here's the complete rule for every cell (i, j):

dp[0][0] = 0
dp[i][0] = i × t_d  (delete everything)    dp[0][j] = j × t_i  (insert everything)

dp[i][j] = min of:
  dp[i−1][j] + t_d  delete
  dp[i][j−1] + t_i  insert
  dp[i−1][j−1] + (0 if match else t_r)  keep / replace
  dp[i−2][j−2] + t_e  swap (only if the crossing pattern matches, and i,j ≥ 2)

The final answer is dp[len(A)][len(B)].

Why does 2×t_e ≥ t_i + t_d matter so much?

Our recipe only ever swaps one pair of neighbors per step. But what if swapping were amazingly cheap — say t_e = 1 while t_i = t_d = 10? Then you could "moonwalk" a ball two spots over using two cheap swaps (cost 2) instead of a delete-and-insert (cost 20) — and a ball could keep moonwalking further and further for almost free. Our simple recipe never considers chains of swaps like that, so it would compute an answer that's too expensive — wrong.

The guarantee 2×t_e ≥ t_i + t_d rules this out. It says two swaps (cost 2×t_e) are never cheaper than one delete plus one insert (cost t_i + t_d). So it's never worth chaining swaps to walk a ball a long distance — a single adjacent swap is always as far as it's ever worth going. That's exactly what lets our one-swap-per-step recipe be trusted to give the true minimum.

LEVEL 6

Writing it in Python

Here's a direct translation of the Level 5 recipe into code. It builds the whole grid, row by row.

sequence_of_balls.py
ti, td, tr, te = map(int, input().split())
A = input()
B = input()
n, m = len(A), len(B)

dp = [[0] * (m + 1) for _ in range(n + 1)]
for i in range(1, n + 1):
    dp[i][0] = i * td
for j in range(1, m + 1):
    dp[0][j] = j * ti

for i in range(1, n + 1):
    for j in range(1, m + 1):
        cost_replace = 0 if A[i - 1] == B[j - 1] else tr
        best = min(
            dp[i - 1][j] + td,
            dp[i][j - 1] + ti,
            dp[i - 1][j - 1] + cost_replace,
        )
        if (i >= 2 and j >= 2
                and A[i - 1] == B[j - 2]
                and A[i - 2] == B[j - 1]):
            best = min(best, dp[i - 2][j - 2] + te)
        dp[i][j] = best

print(dp[n][m])

A note on going big

Strings can be up to 4,000 balls, which means up to roughly 16 million grid cells. That's fine for a fast language like C++, but plain Python will feel this. If you need to handle the largest inputs, the trick below keeps only two rows of the grid in memory at once instead of the whole thing — the grid only ever looks one and two rows backward, so there's no need to keep the rest around.

Bonus: memory-friendly rolling rows
sequence_of_balls_rolling.py
ti, td, tr, te = map(int, input().split())
A = input()
B = input()
n, m = len(A), len(B)

prev2 = [0] * (m + 1)              # row i-2
prev1 = [j * ti for j in range(m + 1)]  # row i-1 (starts as row 0)

for i in range(1, n + 1):
    curr = [0] * (m + 1)
    curr[0] = i * td
    for j in range(1, m + 1):
        cost_replace = 0 if A[i - 1] == B[j - 1] else tr
        best = min(prev1[j] + td, curr[j - 1] + ti, prev1[j - 1] + cost_replace)
        if (i >= 2 and j >= 2
                and A[i - 1] == B[j - 2]
                and A[i - 2] == B[j - 1]):
            best = min(best, prev2[j - 2] + te)
        curr[j] = best
    prev2, prev1 = prev1, curr

print(prev1[m])

Instead of one giant grid, we only ever keep the two most recently finished rows (prev2, two rows back, and prev1, one row back) plus the row we're currently building (curr). Once a row is finished, it slides down and becomes the new "one row back" for the next iteration.

LEVEL 9

Your turn: practice challenges

Work these out by hand first, then check yourself with the tool above.

Challenge 1: A="ab", B="ba", with all four costs equal to 1. What's the minimum time?

Filling the same 3×3 grid with every cost set to 1: dp[1][1]=1 (replace), dp[1][2]=1 (a matches the 'a' in "ba"), dp[2][1]=1 (b matches the 'b' in "ab"), and finally dp[2][2] = min(replace: 1+1=2, delete+insert: 1+1=2, insert+delete: 1+1=2, swap: dp[0][0]+1 = 1). The swap wins again — answer is 1. Notice this is the boundary case of the rule from Level 5: 2×t_e = 2 and t_i+t_d = 2, so they're exactly equal — the rule allows equality, not just strict inequality.

Challenge 2: A="cat", B="cot", with t_i=t_d=t_r=1 (t_e doesn't matter here). What's the minimum time?

'c' matches 'c', and 't' matches 't' — only the middle ball differs ('a' vs 'o'), and there's no crossed pair anywhere for a swap to help with. A single replace fixes it. Minimum time: 1.

Challenge 3: Why does the swap option only ever look at A[i−1], A[i−2], B[j−1], B[j−2] — the very last two balls of each prefix — instead of checking any two balls anywhere in the strings?

Because dp[i][j] is defined as "the cheapest way to turn the first i balls of A into the first j balls of B" — it's built up prefix by prefix, left to right. Every option for filling dp[i][j] has to connect back to a smaller, already-solved prefix problem. A swap that finishes exactly at position (i, j) can only ever involve the two balls sitting right at the boundary of that prefix — anything earlier in the strings was already decided when we computed the smaller subproblems.

Challenge 4: Suppose (hypothetically!) t_e=1 while t_i=t_d=10, breaking the 2×t_e ≥ t_i+t_d rule. In your own words, explain why our Level 5 recipe could give the wrong answer here.

With t_e this cheap, you could slide a ball two positions over using two swaps for a total cost of 2 — far cheaper than deleting and reinserting it (cost 20). A ball could keep sliding, swap after swap, almost for free, effectively "teleporting" long distances cheaply. Our recipe only ever considers one single adjacent swap per grid cell; it has no way to represent a ball sliding many positions through a chain of swaps. So in this hypothetical world, the true minimum cost could be much lower than what our formula computes — the formula would be provably wrong. This is exactly why the problem guarantees 2×t_e ≥ t_i+t_d: it makes chains of swaps never worthwhile, so a single swap per cell is always enough.

Challenge 5 (harder): Modify the code from Level 6 so it also prints which one of the four tools was used at the final cell (dp[n][m]) to reach the optimal answer.
sequence_of_balls_trace.py
i, j = n, m
cost_replace = 0 if A[i - 1] == B[j - 1] else tr
options = {
    "delete": dp[i - 1][j] + td,
    "insert": dp[i][j - 1] + ti,
    "keep/replace": dp[i - 1][j - 1] + cost_replace,
}
if (i >= 2 and j >= 2
        and A[i - 1] == B[j - 2]
        and A[i - 2] == B[j - 1]):
    options["swap"] = dp[i - 2][j - 2] + te

winner = min(options, key=options.get)
print("Last tool used:", winner, "costing", options[winner])

Doing this at every cell (not just the last one), and remembering which option won each time, is how you'd reconstruct the entire sequence of operations, not just the total cost.
