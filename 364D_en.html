Ghd: The Divisor Only Half Your Numbers Need to Share

You know the GCD — the biggest number that divides every single number in a list. Ghd relaxes that rule: it only needs to divide at least half of them. That small change turns an easy problem into a genuine puzzle — with up to a million numbers as big as a trillion, and just 4 seconds to solve it. The fix involves a coin flip.

No math background needed Randomized Algorithms & GCD Interactive
LEVEL 1

The mission, straight from the judge

You're given n numbers (1 ≤ n ≤ 106), each between 1 and 1012. You need to find the largest number g such that at least half of the numbers in the list are evenly divisible by g. This special value is called the Ghd ("greatest half-divisor").

Straight from the judge

6 2 3 4 5 6 → 3  (3 divides 6, 3, and 6 — that's 3 out of 6 numbers, exactly half)
5 5 6 10 15 → 5  (5 divides 5, 5, 10, and 15 — 4 out of 5 numbers, more than half)

New to GCD?

This page assumes you already know what a greatest common divisor (GCD) is — the biggest number that evenly divides every number in a set. If that's new, the Modified GCD and Maximum GCD pages are quick warm-ups. Ghd is deliberately named to sound like GCD, because it's the same idea with the rules loosened.

LEVEL 2

Ghd vs. GCD: why "half" changes everything

The regular GCD of 6, 2, 3, 4, 5, 6 is just 1 — there's no number bigger than 1 that divides all six of them (5 alone kills any candidate bigger than 1, since nothing else shares a factor with it). But Ghd doesn't need everyone's agreement — it only needs a majority. That's why the answer jumps all the way up to 3: three of the six numbers (6, 3, 6) are happy to be divided by 3, and three out of six is enough.

Here's the brute-force idea, just to pin down exactly what we're computing:

ghd_brute_force.py
n = int(input())
a = list(map(int, input().split()))
need = (n + 1) // 2  # "at least half", rounded up

best = 1
for g in range(1, max(a) + 1):
    count = sum(1 for v in a if v % g == 0)
    if count >= need:
        best = g

print(best)

This is 100% correct — and 100% hopeless for the real constraints. max(a) can be 1012, and for every single candidate g we'd scan through up to a million numbers. That's roughly 1012 × 106 = 1018 operations. A computer doing a billion operations per second would still need decades.

The real challenge

We need to find the answer without ever testing "every possible g." We'll need to be clever about which candidates are even worth checking, and clever about how fast we can check each one.

LEVEL 3

Idea #1: a random guess is a surprisingly good bet

Here's the spark that makes this whole problem solvable. Suppose g is the true Ghd. By definition, at least half of the numbers in the array are multiples of g. Now imagine closing your eyes and pointing at one random number in the array. What's the chance you landed on a multiple of g?

≥ 50%

At least 50-50 — the same odds as a fair coin landing heads! And if your randomly-picked number x is a multiple of g, then g is automatically one of x's divisors (a number that divides a multiple of g and happens to equal g itself is, well, a divisor of that multiple). So: pick a random number, look at its divisors, and there's a coin-flip's chance the true answer is hiding in that list.

Why repeating the coin flip basically guarantees success

One coin flip landing on the right side only 50% of the time isn't good enough. But what if you flip 20 separate coins, and you only need one of them to land right? The chance that all 20 miss is (1/2)20 — about 1 in a million. So: pick 20 different random numbers from the array (instead of just 1), collect the divisors of each, and check all of those candidates. The odds of the true Ghd not showing up among any of them are astronomically small.

Seen randomized tricks like this before?

This "repeat a random guess until failure becomes vanishingly unlikely" strategy shows up all over competitive programming — the GCD Escape Machine and Birthday Paradox pages use related probability reasoning, if you want more practice with this style of thinking.

LEVEL 4

Idea #2: listing one number's divisors is cheap

Level 3 narrowed our search from "every integer up to 1012" down to "the divisors of one specific number." How many divisors can a number up to 1012 have? Even in the most extreme case, a handful of thousand — a tiny fraction of a trillion.

divisors_of.py
def divisors_of(x):
    result = []
    d = 1
    while d * d <= x:
        if x % d == 0:
            result.append(d)
            if d != x // d:
                result.append(x // d)
        d += 1
    return result

This is the same √x shortcut from the Prime Factorization page: divisors always come in pairs (d, x/d), so you only ever need to test candidates up to √x. For x = 1012, √x is just 106 — a loop that finishes in a fraction of a second.

LEVEL 5

Idea #3: counting fast with a GCD fingerprint

We now have, say, a few thousand candidate divisors from one sampled number x. For each candidate d, we need to know: how many numbers in the whole array does d divide? The obvious way — scanning the entire array once per candidate — costs (divisors of x) × n, which can still be too slow once you repeat it for 20 different sampled numbers.

Here's the fix: scan the array just once per sampled number, and let GCD do double duty. For every number v in the array, compute gcd(x, v). Two things are always true about this value:

gcd(x, v)
is always a divisor of x

That's just the definition of GCD — it divides both x and v. So as we scan the array, we can drop each v straight into a "bucket" labeled with the exact divisor of x it landed on, using a dictionary that counts how many numbers landed in each bucket. One pass over the whole array, and every number has been sorted into one of x's divisor-buckets.

Turning buckets into "how many are divisible by d"

Landing in the bucket for exactly d isn't the whole story — if v lands in the bucket for 10, then v is also divisible by 5, 2, and 1 (every divisor of 10). So to find how many array numbers are divisible by some candidate d, add up the bucket counts of d and every multiple of d that is itself a divisor of x. Since we're only ever summing over x's own (small) divisor list, this step stays fast.

Why this is exactly correct

Take any divisor d of x, and any number v from the array with bucket value d2 = gcd(x, v). If d divides d2, then since d2 divides v (by definition of gcd), d divides v too. And going the other way: if d divides both x and v, then d must divide gcd(x,v) = d2, since the gcd is the greatest common divisor and every common divisor of two numbers divides it. So "d divides v" and "d divides v's bucket label" are exactly the same statement — nothing gets missed, and nothing gets double-counted incorrectly.

LEVEL 6

Putting the three ideas together

Here's the full plan, combining Levels 3–5:

  • Repeat about 20 times: pick a random number x from the array (Level 3).
  • List all of x's divisors using the √x shortcut (Level 4).
  • Scan the array once, bucketing every number by gcd(x, v) (Level 5).
  • Check x's divisors from largest to smallest; for each one, sum up bucket counts across its multiples. The first one that reaches "at least half the array" is the best candidate hiding inside this particular x.
  • Keep the largest candidate found across all 20 random picks — that's the answer.
Why this fits inside the time limit

Each of the ~20 trials does one O(n) pass over the array (computing a gcd for every element — fast, since GCD only takes a handful of steps even for 12-digit numbers) plus a small cleanup over just x's own divisor list. Twenty passes over a million numbers, with cheap arithmetic each time, comfortably fits inside 4 seconds — a universe away from Level 2's 1018-operation brute force.

And here's the whole thing as one runnable program:

ghd.py
import math
import random

def divisors_of(x):
    result = []
    d = 1
    while d * d <= x:
        if x % d == 0:
            result.append(d)
            if d != x // d:
                result.append(x // d)
        d += 1
    return result

def ghd(a, trials=20):
    n = len(a)
    need = (n + 1) // 2
    best = 1

    sample_size = min(trials, n)
    for x in random.sample(a, sample_size):
        divs = divisors_of(x)
        count = {d: 0 for d in divs}
        for v in a:
            g = math.gcd(x, v)
            count[g] += 1

        for d in sorted(divs, reverse=True):
            if d <= best:
                break
            total = sum(count[d2] for d2 in divs if d2 % d == 0)
            if total >= need:
                best = d
                break

    return best

n = int(input())
a = list(map(int, input().split()))
print(ghd(a))

LEVEL 10

Your turn: practice challenges

Work these out with pencil and paper first, then check yourself with the tool above.

Challenge 1: Find the Ghd of 8, 12, 18, 25 by hand.

n=4, so we need at least 2 numbers to share a divisor. Try g=6: divides 12 and 18 (2 numbers) — works! Try anything bigger: g=8 only divides 8 (1 number, not enough). g=9 only divides 18. g=12 only divides 12. So no candidate above 6 reaches 2 numbers. Answer: 6.

Challenge 2: Why does the algorithm need to check divisors of a sampled number, instead of just checking the sampled number x itself?

Because x itself might not be the answer — the true Ghd could be a smaller number that x happens to be a multiple of. For example, if x = 12 and the true Ghd is 6, then 6 is a divisor of 12, but 12 itself might not divide "at least half" the array. We have to search through all of x's divisors to find the biggest one that actually satisfies the "at least half" rule.

Challenge 3: In the tool above, set trials to 1 and run the array 9, 9, 9, 2, 3 a bunch of times. Sometimes you'll get a wrong (too small) answer instead of the true Ghd. Explain why, and what the true Ghd actually is.

n=5, need ≥ 3. Three of the five numbers (the three 9's) are divisible by 9, so the true Ghd is 9. But with only 1 random trial, there's a 2/5 chance (40%) of picking 2 or 3 instead of a 9 — and if you pick x=2, its only divisors are 1 and 2, and 2 only divides one number (itself), so the algorithm would wrongly report 1. This is exactly why Level 3 insists on ~20 trials: one unlucky pick can miss the answer entirely, but 20 independent unlucky picks in a row is astronomically unlikely.

Challenge 4 (harder): Explain why gcd(x, v) is guaranteed to divide x, using only the definition of GCD — without appealing to any formula.

By definition, gcd(x, v) is the greatest number that divides both x and v. "Divides both x and v" already includes "divides x" as one of its two requirements — so whatever number gcd(x,v) turns out to be, it must divide x, simply because that was part of the definition it had to satisfy in the first place.

Challenge 5: Modify the Python code so that, instead of stopping at the first divisor of x that reaches "at least half," it prints out every divisor of x along with how many array numbers it divides — useful for double-checking the bucket logic by hand.
ghd_debug.py
def show_all_counts(a, x):
    divs = divisors_of(x)
    count = {d: 0 for d in divs}
    for v in a:
        count[math.gcd(x, v)] += 1

    for d in sorted(divs, reverse=True):
        total = sum(count[d2] for d2 in divs if d2 % d == 0)
        print(f"d={d}: {total} numbers divisible by it")

Same bucket-building step as before, just without the early-exit — printing the running total for every one of x's divisors instead of stopping at the first success.
