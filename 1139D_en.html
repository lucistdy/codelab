Steps to One: The GCD Escape Machine

Vivek keeps pulling a lever that spits out a random number from 1 to m, appending it to a list. As soon as the greatest common divisor of everything in the list becomes 1, he escapes. On average, how many numbers does he need to pull before escaping?

No math background needed Probability & number theory Interactive
LEVEL 1

The mission, straight from the judge

Vivek starts with an empty list and a fixed number m. He repeats this loop:

1. pull lever
random x in [1, m]
→
2. append x
list grows by 1
→
3. check gcd
gcd == 1 ? escape! : repeat

He wants to know: on average, how long will the list be when he finally escapes? This is called the expected length.

Straight from the judge

m=1 → expected length 1 (only number ever pulled is 1, gcd is 1 immediately).
m=2 → expected length 2.
m=4 → output 333333338 (a weird-looking number — more on that below!).

Wait, why such a huge, weird answer for m=4?

The true expected length for m=4 is actually the fraction 7/3 — not a whole number at all! Since computers can't print exact fractions with huge denominators in general, the judge asks for the fraction encoded as a single integer using modular arithmetic (we'll fully unlock this trick in Level 8). Keep that fraction, 7/3, in mind — we'll arrive at it by hand later in this page.

LEVEL 2

Warm-up: what does "expected value" even mean?

Imagine flipping a fair coin over and over until it lands on heads. Sometimes you get heads on flip 1 (lucky!). Sometimes you need 2 flips, or 3, or more. Expected value just means: if you played this game thousands of times and averaged the number of flips, what number would you land on?

The math for this exact "keep going until success" situation is a classic: with a fair coin (50% chance of heads each flip), the expected number of flips is exactly 2. That's not a coincidence — it's precisely mirrored in sample 2 above! When m=2, Vivek is really just flipping a coin: each pull is either 1 (escape immediately) or 2 (keep going), each with 50% chance. The judge's own note spells out the sum:

1×½ + 2×¼ + 3×⅛ + … = 2

Why this matters

This "add up length × probability of that exact length" approach works, but it requires knowing the probability of every single possible length (1, 2, 3, 4, … forever). That's a lot of terms to add up directly. Level 3 shows a shortcut that sidesteps this entirely.

LEVEL 3

The shortcut: count how many rounds are "still going"

Here's a neat trick for these "keep going until something happens" games. Instead of asking "what's the exact final length," ask a simpler question over and over: "is the game still going after k pulls?" Then add up the answer (as 1 for yes, 0 for no) across every possible k, starting from k=0.

expected length = P(still going after 0 pulls) + P(still going after 1 pull) + P(still going after 2 pulls) + …

Let's sanity-check this on the coin-flip game from Level 2. "Still going after 0 flips" is always true (probability 1, you haven't even flipped yet). "Still going after 1 flip" means that flip was tails (probability ½). "Still going after 2 flips" means both were tails (probability ¼). And so on:

after 0
1
after 1
1/2
after 2
1/4
after 3
1/8

1 + ½ + ¼ + ⅛ + … = 2 — same answer as before, but computed a completely different way! This "still going" trick turns out to be much easier to work with for Vivek's game, because "still going" has a clean description in terms of gcd, which we tackle next.

LEVEL 4

When is Vivek's game "still going"?

After k pulls, Vivek's game is still going exactly when the gcd of those k numbers is not yet 1. And a group of numbers has a gcd bigger than 1 exactly when all of them share some common factor — some number d ≥ 2 that divides every one of them.

So: "still going after k pulls" is the same event as "there exists some d ≥ 2 that divides all k of the randomly pulled numbers." The next two levels figure out how to compute the probability of that.

A concrete peek ahead

For m=4, if Vivek pulls two 2's and a 4, the gcd is gcd(2,2,4) = 2 — still going, because every number pulled so far is a multiple of 2. The moment a number shows up that isn't a multiple of 2 (like pulling a 1 or a 3), the shared factor is broken and the gcd could drop to 1.

LEVEL 5

The chance that every pull is a multiple of d

Out of the numbers 1 to m, how many are multiples of d? Exactly ⌊m/d⌋ of them (that's integer division — "how many times does d fit into m"). So the probability that a single random pull is a multiple of d is:

pd = ⌊m/d⌋ / m

For k independent pulls, the probability that every single one is a multiple of d is just pd multiplied by itself k times: pdk. Summing this over all k ≥ 0 gives a classic geometric series:

1 + pd + pd2 + pd3 + … = 1 / (1 − pd)

If there were only ONE possible shared factor

If "still going" simply meant "every pull is a multiple of this one specific d," we'd already be done: the expected length contribution would just be 1/(1−pd). The catch is that "still going" means multiples of any d ≥ 2, and different values of d overlap with each other — which brings us to the trickiest (and most fun) part of this problem.

LEVEL 6

The overlap problem, and the Mobius fix

Here's the trap: you might think "probability the gcd isn't 1" equals "sum, over every d ≥ 2, of the probability all pulls are multiples of d." But that overcounts. A set of numbers that are all multiples of 6 gets counted once under d=2, once under d=3, and once under d=6 — triple-counted!

You may have seen this overlap problem before in a simpler form: to count numbers from 1–30 divisible by 2 or by 3, you can't just add "multiples of 2" (15 of them) and "multiples of 3" (10 of them) — you'd double-count multiples of 6 (5 of them), so you have to subtract them back out: 15 + 10 − 5 = 20. That's the inclusion–exclusion principle.

When there could be many overlapping factors at once (not just two), mathematicians use a clever set of correction weights called the Mobius function, written μ(n):

μ(1) = 1
the empty case, always +1
μ(n) = (−1)k
if n is a product of k different primes (like 6=2×3, or 30=2×3×5)
μ(n) = 0
if any prime divides n more than once (like 4=2×2, or 12=2×2×3)

So μ(2) = −1, μ(3) = −1, μ(6) = +1 (two different primes), μ(4) = 0 (2 repeats), μ(12) = 0 (2 repeats). These signs are exactly the "+1, then subtract the overlap" pattern from inclusion–exclusion, generalized to work automatically no matter how many factors overlap at once. With μ in hand, the exact (no overcounting!) probability that a group of k random numbers has gcd not equal to 1 is:

P(gcd ≠ 1) = − ∑d=2m μ(d) × pdk

LEVEL 7

Putting it all together: the final formula

Now combine Level 3's "still going" sum with Level 6's overcount-free probability, and sum the geometric series from Level 5 (starting from k=1, since the k=0 term was already handled separately as the guaranteed "+1" for the very first pull):

expected length = 1 + ∑d=2m (−μ(d)) × ⌊m/d⌋ / (m − ⌊m/d⌋)

That's it — one clean formula covering every case. Let's verify it catches the samples we already know:

expected length = 1 + 1 + 1/3 = 7/3 — exactly the fraction from Level 1!
LEVEL 8

Fractions in disguise: why 333333338?

We just found the true answer for m=4 is 7/3. But the judge wants a single whole number, and computers generally can't store a fraction with an enormous, unpredictable denominator exactly. The fix: do all arithmetic modulo a large prime, P = 1,000,000,007, and represent "divide by 3" as "multiply by the number that undoes multiplying by 3", called the modular inverse of 3.

Thanks to a famous shortcut called Fermat's little theorem, whenever P is prime, the modular inverse of any number x (not a multiple of P) is just:

x−1 mod P = xP−2 mod P

In Python, pow(x, P-2, P) computes this instantly (fast exponentiation makes light work of even a huge exponent like P-2). So 7/3 mod P becomes (7 × pow(3, P-2, P)) % P:

python3
>>> P = 1_000_000_007
>>> (7 * pow(3, P - 2, P)) % P
333333338
The takeaway

Every time you see a strange, huge number as a Codeforces answer for a probability/counting problem, it's almost always an ordinary fraction wearing a disguise: P × Q−1 mod (109+7). Once you know the trick, the "weirdness" disappears completely.

LEVEL 9

Writing it in Python

Two ingredients: a way to compute μ(d) for every d up to m, and the final sum from Level 7. Here's the simplest version, using trial division to factor each number and decide its Mobius value:

steps_to_one.py
MOD = 1_000_000_007

def mobius(n):
    result = 1
    p = 2
    while p * p <= n:
        if n % p == 0:
            n //= p
            if n % p == 0:
                return 0  # p divides n twice -> mu is 0
            result = -result
        p += 1
    if n > 1:
        result = -result  # one leftover prime factor
    return result

m = int(input())
answer = 1
for d in range(2, m + 1):
    mu = mobius(d)
    if mu == 0:
        continue
    fd = m // d
    term = (-mu * fd) % MOD
    term = term * pow(m - fd, MOD - 2, MOD) % MOD
    answer = (answer + term) % MOD

print(answer)

A note on going big

m can be up to 100,000. Trial-dividing every single number up to m works, but it repeats a lot of factoring work. A faster approach precomputes every μ(d) for d up to m all at once, using a "linear sieve" — the same family of technique as the Sieve of Eratosthenes, adapted to track prime factor counts instead of just marking composites.

Bonus: a linear sieve for every Mobius value at once
steps_to_one_sieve.py
MOD = 1_000_000_007
m = int(input())

mu = [1] * (m + 1)
is_composite = [False] * (m + 1)
primes = []
for i in range(2, m + 1):
    if not is_composite[i]:
        primes.append(i)
        mu[i] = -1
    for p in primes:
        if i * p > m:
            break
        is_composite[i * p] = True
        if i % p == 0:
            mu[i * p] = 0
            break
        mu[i * p] = -mu[i]

answer = 1
for d in range(2, m + 1):
    if mu[d] == 0:
        continue
    fd = m // d
    term = (-mu[d] * fd) % MOD
    term = term * pow(m - fd, MOD - 2, MOD) % MOD
    answer = (answer + term) % MOD

print(answer)

This computes every Mobius value from 2 to m in one single pass, instead of re-factoring each number from scratch — the difference that makes it comfortably fast even at m = 100,000.

LEVEL 11

Your turn: practice challenges

Work these out by hand first, then check yourself with the tool above.

Challenge 1: Compute the expected length for m=3 by hand using Level 7's formula.

Divisors to check: d=2 and d=3. μ(2)=−1, ⌊3/2⌋=1, contribution = 1 × 1/(3−1) = 1/2. μ(3)=−1, ⌊3/3⌋=1, contribution = 1 × 1/(3−1) = 1/2. Total: 1 + 1/2 + 1/2 = 2. The expected length for m=3 is exactly 2.

Challenge 2: Why does the formula completely skip every d with μ(d)=0, like d=4, 8, 9, 12?

μ(d)=0 happens exactly when d has a repeated prime factor (like 4=2×2 or 12=2×2×3). In the inclusion–exclusion process, the "being a multiple of d" event for such a d is already fully accounted for by its squarefree divisors (its prime factors without repetition) — including it again would double-correct and throw off the total. The Mobius function elegantly sets its own weight to exactly 0 in precisely these cases, so the sum doesn't need any special-casing — you can just skip it.

Challenge 3: In Level 7, why does the sum start the geometric series contribution from k=1 rather than k=0, even though Level 3's "still going" sum itself starts at k=0?

The k=0 term ("still going after 0 pulls") is always true with probability 1 no matter what — it doesn't depend on gcds or divisors at all, since no numbers have been pulled yet. That's exactly the guaranteed "+1" sitting out in front of the formula. The Mobius sum only needs to describe what happens for k=1, 2, 3, … (after at least one pull), which is why its geometric series starts at k=1.

Challenge 4: Compute μ(30). (Hint: factor 30 first.)

30 = 2 × 3 × 5 — three different prime factors, none repeated. So μ(30) = (−1)3 = −1.

Challenge 5 (harder): The judge guarantees m ≤ 100,000. Roughly how many total "prime factor checks" does the linear sieve from Level 9 perform, and why is that so much faster than trial-dividing every number separately?

The linear sieve visits each number from 2 to m exactly once as the outer loop variable, and each composite number gets marked by exactly one prime (its smallest prime factor) in the inner loop — so the total work is proportional to m itself (technically O(m)), around 100,000 operations for the largest input. Trial division, by contrast, refactors every single number independently from scratch, doing roughly √d work per number d — about √100,000 ≈ 316 per number, or roughly 30 million operations total. Both finish well within the time limit for this problem's constraints, but the sieve scales far better if m were ever pushed much higher.
