The Birthday Paradox: Taming a Fraction With 1018 in It

In a year with 2n days, ZS interviews k people. What's the probability that at least two of them share a birthday? The catch: n and k can be as large as 1018 — far too big to compute anything the direct way.

No math background needed Probability & number theory Interactive
LEVEL 1

The mission, straight from the judge

Udayland has 2n days in a year. ZS interviews k people, each with a birthday chosen uniformly at random from those 2n days. He wants the probability that at least two of them share a birthday, written as a fraction A/B in lowest terms (A and B share no common factor), and then printed mod (106+3).

Straight from the judge

n=3, k=2 → A=1, B=8  (8 days, 2 people, probability 1/8)
n=1, k=3 → A=1, B=1  (only 2 days but 3 people — a match is guaranteed!)
n=4, k=3 → A=23, B=128

Why is this hard?

n and k can each be up to 1018. That means 2n could be a number with hundreds of quintillion digits — you couldn't even store it, let alone compute a probability with it directly. And yet the judge wants an exact, fully-reduced fraction. This page is about the clever shortcuts that make that possible.

LEVEL 2

Wait, what's the Birthday Paradox?

Here's a classic party trick: get 23 random people in a room, and there's already about a 50% chance that two of them share a birthday — even though there are 365 days in a year and only 23 people! Most people find this shocking at first, since 23 feels tiny compared to 365.

The trick to understanding it: you're not checking whether someone shares your specific birthday (that really would need much more than 23 people). You're checking whether any two of the 23 people match each other — and with 23 people, there are 23×22/2 = 253 different pairs of people who could potentially match. 253 chances for a coincidence adds up fast!

LEVEL 3

The formula: flip the question around

"At least two people share a birthday" is awkward to compute directly — there are tons of ways that could happen. It's much easier to compute the opposite: the probability that everyone has a completely different birthday, and then subtract from 1.

Imagine interviewing people one at a time, with m = 2n possible days. The 1st person can have any birthday (probability 1 of "no clash yet"). The 2nd person must avoid the 1st person's day: probability (m−1)/m. The 3rd person must avoid 2 taken days: probability (m−2)/m. And so on, for all k people:

P(no match) = m⁄m × (m−1)⁄m × (m−2)⁄m × … × (m−k+1)⁄m

P(at least one match) = 1 − P(no match)

Checking it against sample 1

For n=3, k=2: m=8. P(no match) = 8/8 × 7/8 = 7/8. So P(match) = 1 − 7/8 = 1/8. Matches A=1, B=8 exactly!

LEVEL 4

When is a match 100% guaranteed?

If you try to interview more people than there are days in the year, a shared birthday isn't just likely — it's certain. This is the classic pigeonhole principle: if you have more pigeons than pigeonholes, at least one hole must contain two pigeons.

Here, "pigeons" are people and "pigeonholes" are days. If k > 2n, the answer is instantly P = 1, i.e. A=1, B=1 — no computation needed at all!

Checking it against sample 2

For n=1, k=3: there are only 21=2 days but 3 people. Since 3 > 2, a match is guaranteed. Output: 1 1. Exactly what the judge expects.

A useful shortcut for huge n

Since k ≤ 1018, once n ≥ 60, 2n is already bigger than 260 ≈ 1.15 × 1018, which is already bigger than any possible k. So this "guaranteed match" case can only ever happen when n is small (under 60) — meaning 2n is small enough to actually compute and compare directly against k, no huge-number trouble involved.

LEVEL 5

The real problem: numbers too big to even write down

Once we know a match isn't guaranteed (so k ≤ 2n), we still need to compute Level 3's formula — and that's where things get scary. 2n with n up to 1018 is a number so large it would take more storage than exists on Earth to write out in full. And k itself can be up to 1018 terms in that product — far too many to multiply one at a time.

So we need to find shortcuts that let us compute the final answer without ever computing the astronomically large intermediate numbers along the way. The next few levels build up exactly those shortcuts, one at a time.

LEVEL 6

The big insight: the answer's denominator is always a power of 2

Look again at Level 3's formula for P(no match): its denominator is mk = (2n)k = 2n×k. That's it — a pure power of 2, with no other prime factors anywhere in it!

When you reduce a fraction to lowest terms, you can only cancel out factors that the numerator and denominator share. Since the denominator here is 100% made of 2's, the only thing that can ever get cancelled is 2's. That means: no matter how ugly the numerator looks, the fully-reduced fraction's denominator will always come out as some power of 2.

Why this changes everything

This turns our problem into a much narrower question: exactly how many factors of 2 does the numerator contain? If we can count that single number, we instantly know exactly how much to cancel — and we never have to deal with any other prime factor at all.

LEVEL 7

Counting the 2's: Legendre's formula

The numerator of P(no match) is 2n × (2n−1) × (2n−2) × … × (2n−k+1). The very first term, 2n, contributes exactly n factors of 2, since it's a pure power of 2. What about the rest?

Here's a neat fact: since i is always much smaller than 2n in this problem, the number of factors of 2 in (2n − i) turns out to be exactly the number of factors of 2 in i itself, for every i from 1 to k−1. So the total count of 2's hiding in all those terms combined equals the number of factors of 2 hiding inside 1 × 2 × 3 × … × (k−1), in other words inside (k−1)!.

Counting factors of 2 in a factorial has a classic shortcut called Legendre's formula: just keep dividing by increasing powers of 2 and adding up the whole-number results.

v2(m!) = ⌊m/2⌋ + ⌊m/4⌋ + ⌊m/8⌋ + …

Worked example: v2(10!)

⌊10/2⌋ + ⌊10/4⌋ + ⌊10/8⌋ + ⌊10/16⌋ = 5 + 2 + 1 + 0 = 8. So 10! is divisible by 28 = 256, but not by 29. (Double-check if you like: 2,4,6,8,10 each contribute at least one factor of 2 — that's 5. Then 4 and 8 contribute an extra factor each — that's 2 more. Then 8 contributes yet another — that's 1 more. Total: 5+2+1=8.)

So the total power of 2 in Level 3's numerator is:

V = n + v2((k−1)!)

LEVEL 8

A second pigeonhole trick: stop multiplying early

We still need the numerator's odd part (everything left over after all the 2's are pulled out), reduced modulo the prime p = 1,000,003. The naive plan — multiply out all k terms one by one, mod p — is a problem again if k is up to 1018.

Here's the rescue: p itself is "only" about a million. The numerator's terms are k consecutive whole numbers counting downward. Once you line up p or more consecutive integers, by the pigeonhole principle, their remainders when divided by p must cover every single remainder from 0 to p−1 at least once — including remainder 0! That means one of those terms is an exact multiple of p.

The shortcut

So: if k ≥ p, we know for certain that the product (mod p) is exactly 0 — without checking a single term. We only ever need to actually run the multiplication loop when k < p, meaning at most about a million iterations, ever — regardless of how astronomically large k is allowed to be.

LEVEL 9

Removing the 2's with a modular inverse

We now have two mod-p ingredients: NumMod (the full numerator mod p, computed the fast way from Level 8) and V (the exact count of 2's to remove, from Level 7). To "divide out" 2V under modular arithmetic, we multiply by its modular inverse instead — the same Fermat's-little-theorem trick used elsewhere in this series:

(2V)−1 mod p = (2V)p−2 mod p

Multiplying NumMod by this inverse gives us OddMod — the numerator's "everything but the 2's" part, reduced mod p. From here:

B = 2E,  where E = n×k − V  (the leftover power of 2 in the denominator after cancelling)
A = B − OddMod  (since P(match) = 1 − OddMod/B = (B−OddMod)/B)

Why A and B are automatically coprime

Since OddMod came from a number with all factors of 2 already stripped out, it's odd. Subtracting an odd number from an even power of 2 (as long as E ≥ 1) always gives an odd result. So A is odd and B is a pure power of 2 — they share no common factor greater than 1, exactly satisfying the judge's "already reduced" requirement.

LEVEL 10

Writing it in Python

Time to assemble everything from Levels 4–9 into working code. Python's built-in arbitrary-size integers and 3-argument pow(base, exp, mod) (which handles even gigantic exponents efficiently) make this remarkably short.

birthday_paradox.py
MOD = 1_000_003

def v2_factorial(m):
    total = 0
    p = 2
    while p <= m:
        total += m // p
        p *= 2
    return total

n, k = map(int, input().split())

# Level 4: is a match guaranteed?
if n < 63 and k > (1 << n):
    print(1, 1)
else:
    # Level 7: total power of 2 in the numerator
    V = n + v2_factorial(k - 1)

    # Level 8: numerator mod p, short-circuited past a million terms
    n_mod = pow(2, n, MOD)
    if k >= MOD:
        num_mod = 0
    else:
        num_mod = 1
        for i in range(k):
            num_mod = num_mod * ((n_mod - i) % MOD) % MOD

    # Level 9: strip out the 2's using a modular inverse
    inv_2V = pow(pow(2, V, MOD), MOD - 2, MOD)
    odd_mod = num_mod * inv_2V % MOD

    E = n * k - V
    B = pow(2, E, MOD)
    A = (B - odd_mod) % MOD

    print(A, B)
if n < 63 and k > (1 << n): print(1, 1)

Challenge 1: Compute v2(6!) using Legendre's formula, and double check it by listing out the factors of 2 in 1,2,3,4,5,6 directly.

Legendre: ⌊6/2⌋ + ⌊6/4⌋ + ⌊6/8⌋ = 3 + 1 + 0 = 4. Direct check: 2 contributes one 2, 4 contributes two 2's (2×2), 6 contributes one 2 — total 1+2+1 = 4. They match: 6! = 720 = 24 × 45.

Challenge 2: For n=2, k=5, is a shared birthday guaranteed? What should the output be?

22 = 4 days, but 5 people — since 5 > 4, a match is guaranteed by the pigeonhole principle. Output: 1 1, with no further computation needed.

Challenge 3: Why does the problem specifically check "n < 63" before computing 1 << n, instead of just always computing 2n and comparing?

Because n can be as large as 1018, and 2n for such an n would be an unimaginably large number — way too big to compute, store, or compare against anything in any reasonable time or memory. But since k ≤ 1018 < 260, the moment n reaches 60 or so, we already know for certain that 2n > k without needing to compute the actual value — so we simply skip the "guaranteed match" branch and move on to the modular-arithmetic approach, which handles huge n just fine.

Challenge 4: Explain in your own words why, once k ≥ 1,000,003, the numerator's remainder mod p is guaranteed to be exactly 0 — without doing any multiplication.

The numerator is a product of k consecutive descending whole numbers: 2n, 2n−1, 2n−2, …. Any p consecutive whole numbers, when you look at their remainders after dividing by p, must include every possible remainder from 0 to p−1 exactly once (they can't skip any, since they're consecutive and there are exactly p of them). Since 0 is one of those possible remainders, at least one of the terms must be an exact multiple of p — and multiplying anything by a multiple of p gives a result that's also a multiple of p, i.e. 0 modulo p. Once k is at least p, we're guaranteed to have at least p consecutive terms in the product, so this always applies.

Challenge 5 (harder): Modify the Level 10 code so that it works even if the input uses n=0 (meaning there's only 1 day in the year). What should happen, and does the existing code already handle it correctly?

With n=0, there's 20=1 day in the year, and since k ≥ 2 is guaranteed by the problem's own constraints, we'd have k ≥ 2 > 1 = 2n — so the pigeonhole check n < 63 and k > (1 << n) catches this immediately and prints 1 1, correctly reflecting that with only one possible day, any 2 or more people are guaranteed to share it. No extra code changes needed — it's already handled by the very first check.
